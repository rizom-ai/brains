The case for collective intelligence.

What stands in the way

Memory without a home settles wherever it is cheapest to keep.

People and organizations rarely lose what they know all at once. It drains away, because their memory has no home of its own: who knows what, why a decision was made, whose judgment settles a question. For a long time that meant a few indispensable people, who take it with them when they leave. Now it increasingly means a model owned by one company, which amplifies whatever it finds, confusion included. The two look different, but they are symptoms of the same gap.

None of this is a law of nature. How people remember and decide together is built, by habits and by tools, so it can be built differently. What is missing is a commons: memory that keeps its reasons, and agreements that no one in the middle owns.

The change runs through two steps

Each with a technical and a social side.

01 · Memory they own

Each person and organization has a Brain: their memory in open files they own, which people and their AI tools can work from. It keeps the map of who knows what, whose judgment counts and why things were decided, while lived experience stays with the people who hold it.

A practice makes writing down reasons a habit. Knowledge stops depending on who is still in the room.

02 · Connection without a center

An open standard lets independent parties find each other by expertise, share what they choose and keep a record of their agreements, signed by both and held by each.

A charter sets how disagreements are settled. The Foundation holds the rules, never the parties' memory, and anyone can host their own and leave.

Mission

The Rizom Foundation works to decenter AI, moving intelligence back to the people and organizations it comes from.

It gives them memory of their own and connections that no one in the middle owns, so that what they know depends neither on one company's model nor on a few indispensable people. The Foundation researches and builds that infrastructure, technical and social, and holds it as a commons that cannot be sold.

Five assumptions, tested in public

Each has a test and its thresholds, published before the test runs.

  1. A1Memory that people own strengthens their map of who knows what, and keeps it intact when people leave.
  2. A2People will hold their own memory once it costs no more effort than handing it over.
  3. A3Connection without a center can be made as easy as a platform, so parties do not drift back to one.
  4. A4Others can learn the method through apprenticeship, without its author in the room.
  5. A5Organizations pay for the method and hosting though the software is free, and a share of that funds the commons.
Each assumption links to its test and, later, its results. None is published yet, so the links are not drawn here.

Why a foundation

Infrastructure people depend on should not belong to whoever happens to sell it.

The Foundation owns the code, the method and the Rizom name. The core stays under AGPL, the SDK and protocols under Apache. Rizom B.V. runs hosting and client work under license, cannot take the commons with it, and pays 25% of every engagement back. An independent board majority decides on the license, the name and the company, and the Foundation takes no investors.

No one has to take Rizom on trust. Anyone can read the code, run it for free and leave with their memory. Hosting differs from self-hosting by service, never by what the software can do.
